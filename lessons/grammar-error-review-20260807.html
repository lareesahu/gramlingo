Conditionals: First & Second

You mixed up the first conditional (real future) and second conditional (hypothetical imagination). They share the "if" structure — but the verb forms are completely different.

🧪 First Conditional — Real, Possible Future

Use when the outcome is likely or possible. The condition is realistic.

if + present simple , will + base verb
  • "If it rains tomorrow, we will stay at home."
  • "If she studies, she will pass the exam."
  • "If you call me, I will answer."
⚠️ Your Error #2 — First Conditional
Q: If it ___ tomorrow, we ___ at home.
You: rains… stay
Fix: rains… will stay
💡 The main clause needs will. "Stay" alone is the bare infinitive — it's missing the future marker. Think: if-clause = present, result-clause = will + verb.

🌌 Second Conditional — Hypothetical, Unreal

Use for imaginary, unlikely, or impossible situations. You're daydreaming or speculating.

if + past simple , would + base verb
  • "If I were rich, I would travel the world." (I'm not rich — I'm imagining)
  • "If she knew the answer, she would tell us."
  • "If we lived in Paris, we would speak French every day."

⚡ Key detail: Use "were" for all persons in the if-clause (formal/written English). "If I were…" / "If she were…" / "If it were…" — even though "I was" is normal past tense, the subjunctive "were" marks the hypothetical mood.

⚠️ Your Error #1 — Second Conditional
Q: If I ___ rich, I ___ travel the world.
You: am… will
Fix: were… would
💡 "am…will" is present + future — that's the first conditional pattern. But the sentence imagines being rich (unreal), so it needs the second conditional: past-form verb + "would".
❌ First Conditional (wrong for this meaning)
If I am rich, I will travel the world.
Sounds like: "I might actually become rich soon."
✅ Second Conditional (correct)
If I were rich, I would travel the world.
Sounds like: "I'm not rich — but I'm imagining."

Past Simple vs Present Perfect

This is one of the hardest distinctions in English. The key question: is there a connection to the present?

📐 The Core Rule

Past Simple
Action is finished and disconnected from now.
Time markers: yesterday, in 2010, last week, when I was…
Present Perfect
Action has relevance or result now.
Time markers: just, already, yet, since, ever, never
⚠️ Your Error #3 — Shakespeare (Past Simple)
Q: Shakespeare ___ many famous plays.
You: has written
Fix: wrote
💡 Shakespeare is dead. His play-writing is a completed chapter of history — it has zero connection to the present. Dead people + finished historical events = past simple. Always.
⚠️ Your Error #4 — Lost Keys (Present Perfect)
Q: I ___ my keys. Can you help me find them?
You: lose
Fix: have lost
💡 The second sentence "Can you help me find them?" is the giveaway. The losing happened in the past, but the result is alive right now: the keys are still missing. Present perfect = past action + present consequence.
❌ Wrong
I lose my keys. Can you help me?
Present simple = habit/routine. "I lose my keys every week." Not this situation.
✅ Correct
I have lost my keys. Can you help me?
Past action → present problem (keys still gone).
🔑 The "since" signal: When you see "since" + a time point (since 2020, since I was a child, since Monday), the verb must be present perfect. No exceptions in standard English.
⚠️ Your Error #9 — Paris (Present Perfect + since)
Q: We ___ to Paris three times since 2020.
You: went
Fix: have been
💡 "Since 2020" = a time period that continues up to now. More trips could happen. "Went" locks the trips in the past. "Have been" keeps the door open to the present. Also: "have gone" would mean you're still there — "have been" = went and returned.
❌ Wrong
We went to Paris three times since 2020.
✅ Correct
We have been to Paris three times since 2020.
VerbWhen to UseExample
Past SimpleCompleted past, no present link. Dead people. Finished eras.Shakespeare wrote plays.

3

Future Forms: Scheduled Events

🚂 Timetabled Events → Present Simple

When an event is on a fixed schedule — trains, planes, classes, movie times, TV programs — English uses the present simple for future meaning.

The [scheduled thing] + present simple + future time
  • "The train leaves at 6 PM per the schedule."
  • "The flight departs at 7:30 AM tomorrow."
  • "My class starts at 9 AM on Monday."
  • "The movie begins at 8 PM."
🧠 Why not "is leaving"? Present continuous ("is leaving") is for personal plans and arrangements, not fixed external schedules. "I am leaving at 6 PM" = my personal plan. "The train leaves at 6 PM" = the railway company's timetable. The schedule exists independently of anyone's personal intention.
⚠️ Your Error #5 — The Train
Q: The train ___ at 6 PM per the schedule.
You: is leaving
Fix: leaves
💡 "Per the schedule" is the dead giveaway. That phrase signals a timetabled event → present simple.

Relative Clauses: No Double Pronouns

🔗 One Pronoun, One Job

When a relative pronoun (that, which, who, whom) refers to the object, it replaces the object pronoun. You cannot have both.

The report that Anna wrote it is ready.

"that" already refers to "the report". Adding "it" creates a duplicate reference — the same object is named twice. English only needs it once.

⚠️ Your Error #6 — Relative Clause
Q: Fix: The report that Anna wrote is ready. Which word is extra?
You: Anna
Fix: it — the sentence is already correct as written! There is no "it" in it.
💡 "Anna" is the subject of the relative clause — she's doing the writing. You can't remove her. The sentence as given is already grammatical: "The report that Anna wrote is ready." If someone had written "The report that Anna wrote it is ready" — then "it" would be the extra word.
🔎 How to spot it: Find the relative pronoun (that, which, who). Trace what noun it replaces. If there's a second pronoun (it, him, her, them) referring to the same noun — delete it. The relative pronoun already handles the job.
❌ Wrong (double pronoun)
The book that I bought it is great.
✅ Correct
The book that I bought is great.
❌ Wrong (double pronoun)
The woman who I met her is a doctor.
✅ Correct
The woman who I met is a doctor.

Prepositions: at vs in

Prepositions are tough because languages divide space differently. Here's the English logic for hotels and corners.

📍 The "at" = point, "in" = enclosure rule

at — a POINT or location
Think of the location as a dot on a map. You're not emphasizing being inside the walls.
in — INSIDE an enclosure
Think of being physically surrounded by walls, boundaries, or an area.
⚠️ Your Error #7 — Hotel
Q: Fill: We stayed ___ a hotel.
You: in
Fix: at
💡 "Stay at a hotel" is a fixed collocation in English. When you're a guest using the hotel as temporary accommodation, it's "at." "In a hotel" would emphasize the physical interior — like "I'm waiting in the hotel lobby."
⚠️ Your Error #8 — Corner
Q: Fill: He is standing ___ the corner.
You: in
Fix: at
💡 A corner is a point where two streets or walls meet. Standing at that point = "at the corner." "In the corner" means inside the room, in the corner area (surrounded by walls on two sides). Different meaning entirely!
✅ at the corner (street)
The café is at the corner of Main & Oak. Meet me at the corner.
✅ in the corner (room)
The lamp is in the corner of the living room.
